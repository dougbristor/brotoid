The q map

What this map shows

The sphere. Each point is a direction q could point, seen from outside. At the start, i faces you, j is to the right and 1 is at the top. It is the same floor as the flat map (bird | flat) and as fable's Fig 3 (the hand over every direction q can point), wrapped back onto the sphere it was unrolled from. The pink dots are where the crossing cone meets the sphere: one closed wave around it, between 1 and −1, with three crests and three troughs. The back of the sphere is the front turned over, with cyan and orange swapped: q and −q square to the same skin with the hands swapped. In the bird view the ball is a bird over q, and its height is the ball's size r/|q|.

The floor says what a ball of the bird's size would have, if it sat at that q. Dark cyan or dark orange: it has not reached the crossing cone yet, so it has q's own hand. These are Fig 3's colours. Pink: it crosses the cone (a crossing loop). Gold: a weft (2 umbrellas). Pale gold: two wefts. The pink dots are the cone itself, where a ball of size 0 would cross. So with a tiny bird, the floor is Fig 3.

Why the bands are so wide. Seen from 0, the ball covers every direction within α = asin(r/|q|) of q: the white dashed ring. A ball has a crossing exactly when its ring reaches the pink dots. So the pink band is the cone widened by α on each side (check RING1: 100% of the map's cells, at five sizes). On the sphere the ring is a true circle; the flat map stretches it. At r/|q| 0.72, α is 46°, and the band covers 65% of the map. At 0.3 it is 17.5° and 24%. It is not |q|: scaling q and r together only scales the brotoid, so the map depends on r/|q| alone. Gold sits inside pink because, for most q, a weft follows soon after the crossing.

The strip shows the pattern growing. At r/|q| 0.1 the band is a thin seam along the wave (8% of the sphere has a crossing). At 0.3 it is 24%, at 0.5 42%, at 0.72 65%, and at 0.9 96%: almost every direction's ball now crosses the cone.

The journey (folded below) is a guided flight that plays the flow and the map together. At conch i the bird climbs: the ball grows through ① crossing born, ② weft born and ③ r = |q|, and each moment is named on the flow as it happens. Then it flies level to q2: q moves at a fixed ball size, so you watch the floor change under it. At q2 it climbs again: the same three moments, at other sizes. At q3 it climbs once more, and a second weft is born just before ③. It shows that the moments depend on two things, where q points and how big the ball is, and that the map tells you both at once.

How the ball meets the brim

The brim is the crossing cone, det J = 0: the points where squaring flattens one direction. It is a cone through 0 (the pink fan), so it meets the sphere of directions in a curve, and that curve is the pink wave on the q sphere: the q sphere is this picture's unit sphere seen from outside. It turns side-on to q whenever q moves, so 0, the ray through q and the nearest brim ray lie flat in the picture; drag to turn it.

The ball sits at q with radius r. It is cyan where squaring keeps the hand and orange where it mirrors it. The brim is where the colour changes, so the line where the ball's surface goes through the brim is the crossing (gold). Squaring carries that line to the body: it is the same gold line as on the bodies below and on the flow.

Why the bands are so wide. Seen from 0, the ball fills every direction within α = asin(r/|q|) of q (the white sight lines and their ring). θ is the angle from q to the nearest brim direction (the bright pink ray). The ball has a crossing exactly when θ < α. Measured: the size at which the crossing is born, ①, equals |q| sin θ at all 7,200 cells of the map, to 0.001. So the pink band is every q within α of the wave, and α is large for a fat ball: r/|q| 0.3 gives 17°, 0.5 gives 30°, 0.667 (Fig 2's) gives 42°, 0.72 gives 46°. At Fig 2's size the band is 84° wide, about a quarter of the way round the sphere. It is the ball's size on the brim, not |q|: scale q and r together and nothing changes.

The weft comes later, by an amount that depends on where q points. Reaching the brim gives the crossing at once. The weft needs the ball pushed further through, until the squared skin folds through itself. Measured over the map (q within 30° of the brim): the weft is born at 1.0 to 2.5 × the touching size, median 1.4. Near conch i it is only 1.1 ×, so there the weft follows the crossing almost at once. That is why, at Fig 2's q, almost every ball that crosses also has a weft, and the gold fills nearly all of the pink. Elsewhere (q2, q3) the gap is wider and a pink rim shows outside the gold.

Each point of the map is a direction for q (|q| = 1): across is around, up is toward 1, as the sliders. The floor shows what a ball of the bird's radius would have at every q at once: dark cyan or dark orange = no crossing yet (q's own hand), pink = a crossing but no weft, gold = a weft with its 2 umbrellas, pale gold = two wefts (4 umbrellas). As the bird climbs, the colours flood outward. The thin pink curve is where q itself lies on the crossing cone, so ① comes at r = 0 there. The white line is q's ground track. On the bird's string, ① ② ③ mark this q's three moments; the dashed frame is ③, r = |q|, the same height everywhere. The map repeats itself turned over (lon + 180°, −lat), because q and −q square to the same skin. Drag on the map to move q, use the wheel for r, and drag beside the map to turn the bird's view.

About this page

Figures (fable's pageflow hooks: each has a ⧉ ref that copies a line any agent can find it by; paste it back to insights):

    catchfield's b-splat dust, riding the skin of fable's squared ball (the brotoid's conch, q = i). Each grain lives on the ball around q, is carried through the square, and is drawn as catchfield draws it: Σ = grain²·I + (v·streak)(v·streak)ᵀ, with trails and bloom. A grain is born gold (free). Once the field catches it, it turns cyan where squaring keeps its hand and orange where squaring mirrors it. The skin blocks the dust behind it, as the inv-jbrot cloud does under the body, so you see only the near side. Where the skin passes through itself, the weft is drawn as a pale gold line (Doug's names: crossing (19 October; it was 'join' from 17 October), crossing cone, weft, umbrella).

    Built by insights on 17 October 2026, at Doug's request (“can it not be a surface catchfield”, then “move that to the GPU”, “only facing shows … I want it to be able to show the intersection”). The algebra is copied verbatim from fable's brotoid page: the product (i-roll), the square LAY.sq = (q+δ)² − q², the hand rule (the sign of det J at the input point) and the view. The look is caro's catchfield (random_antenna_control): b-splat dust, Σ = grain²·I + (v·streak)(v·streak)ᵀ, the same light per grain, gold free and cyan or orange caught, persistence, bloom and fog. The field is built from det J on the ball. Flow runs down its gradient, hand curl runs along its contour lines signed by the hand, and swirl is a rigid turn. The grains move on the GPU, one texel per grain, by a GLSL copy of the CPU field; GPU1 checks the copy against the CPU version. Blocking follows the inv-jbrot cloud (ray_cloud_melt): the skin's nearest depth is drawn first. Dust shows within skin depth of it, fades over see into, and is then hidden. The weft is found by drawing the next layer behind the nearest one. A pixel is on the weft when those two layers meet in depth but come from distant points of the ball. Where the skin just curves over its own rim, the two layers come from neighbouring points, so the rim is not marked.
    Comet tails (Doug, 17 October: “the splat paths are good and aesthetic, but not clear … the flow lines themselves feel blurred”). Each grain keeps its last 24 positions on the ball in a ring of texture layers. Every frame, the tail is drawn as a thin tapered line through those positions, carried through the square and projected again. Splat trails fade on screen, so they smear when the picture turns; a comet tail is recomputed from the ball, so it stays sharp. Each point of a tail takes the hand colour where it is, so a tail changes colour where it crosses the crossing. In this look the weft line gets a dark rim, so it reads against bright tails. TAIL1 checks that tail point k is the grain's own position k−1 ticks back. TAIL2 draws one grain's tail alone and checks that its light lies on its CPU path and is brightest at the head end. When the ring is read the wrong way round (the plant), the light still lies on the path, so only the head-versus-end test catches it.
    The umbrellas (Doug, 17 October: “both, start with marking the umbrellas”). An umbrella is where the skin's map stops being smooth: on the crossing, J squashes one direction to nothing, and at an umbrella that squashed direction lies along the ball's surface. They are found where that direction's share along the surface changes sign along the crossing, then polished by Newton's method. UMB1 checks them against fable's two (board 912a84f8ea, found by a different search) and checks that the double curve, found by the independent pair search, ends on them. Standard names: umbrella = Whitney umbrella = cross-cap point = pinch point; the gold line is the double curve.
    The q setter (Doug, 17 October: “add the q setter too … as a PIP TR”) is fable's sphere setter (fable/web/lib/sphere_setter.js), the same one as on the brotoid page. Roll the sphere under the pin to choose q's direction. The pin's length shows the ball size, and the sliders and bookmarks roll it to match.
    The orac b-splat look (Doug, 17 October: “these b-splat dust are really good can we try in the brotoid”) is catchfield_orac's conic at his values: Σ = grain²·I + (v·streak)(v·streak)ᵀ plus the minimum size, all scaled by splat size, with each grain's energy kept constant and capped, so a stretched splat dims and a tiny one never brightens. ORAC1 checks that its light lies on the surface.
    The q map and the journey (Doug, 18 October: “provide an animation of that along with the flow, also show the q-set map journey … marker size or pin height for r, or try the isometric bird's eye view and then show the r as a 'bird' dot with shadow over the map”). Doug picked the bird and the name on the same day: “go with the bird, call it q map”. The journey plays turn_through §5 at the conch, then flies to q2 and q3. The moments it passes are counted by the CodeT atoms (crossing loops from brotoid_join, wefts as brotoid_weft's umbrellas ÷ 2) and named on the flow. The map's floor comes from the moments atom, computed once for every 3° cell (make_qmap_terrain.mjs). A sampled shortcut was tried first and was wrong in 142 of 800 cells, because G's valleys beside its gaps are far narrower than a step there. QMAP1 recomputes random cells live and compares the floor's counts with the atoms'. TOUR1 runs the journey without drawing and checks it names ① ② ③ at the atom's radii. Since 18 October the page's umbrellas, crossing and moments come from those atoms; its first versions are kept as oracle B (ATOM1). Doug's names: crossing (19 October; it was 'join' from 17 October), crossing cone, weft, umbrella.
    Cross weft and self weft (Doug, 18 October: “show both of those types”). Each point of a weft is two ball points, one on each sheet, and each has a hand. Where the hands are opposite, a cyan sheet passes through an orange one: a cross weft, drawn gold. Where they are the same, one hand passes through itself: a self weft, drawn green. brotoid_weft cuts each weft into these stretches, with the switch points found exactly (where one sheet crosses the crossing). Every weft is born cross at its umbrellas and grows a self middle; its hand flips only where the weft runs through 0, at ③ (which hand comes first depends on q: near the real axis the keep hand can come before ③; insights 10-19). The line takes its colour from the atom's nearest stretch. The first rule, the hands of the two skin layers at the pixel, drew part of a cross weft green beside an umbrella: there the sheets meet nearly tangent, the line is wide, and its edge pairs points across the crossing. CROSS2 reads the line's colour back and compares it with the atom; the first rule is its plant.
    Creases (Doug, 18 October: “the pre-crossing is the crease, a crossing is a tear … draw creases on brotoid_flow”; those are the words of 18 October: since 19 October “crossing” names det J = 0, and the crease is the pre-weft). A crease is where a weft will be born: a dip in how far G's midpoints sit from q that r has not reached yet. The cyan ring sits on the ball point nearest that dip, and its number is the gap, how much further r must grow. The skin really folds there: its squash falls in step with the gap and its curvature grows like 1/gap², so the fold sharpens to a knife edge and then goes through itself (the brotoid page). Only creases within 0.5 are ringed. CREASE1 checks the ring against two things it does not use: the skin's own squash minimum, within 3 px; and the two umbrellas born just past the dip, whose midpoint lies on the ring and whose separation shrinks like √(r − h) toward it. Its plant puts the ring on the far side of the ball, and both refuse it. A bright fold line in the dust is not always a crease: a self weft seen side-on looks like one, because both sheets have the same colour.
