0Four words for one number

BROT reads as four words: Bristor, Rotation, Order, Torsion. takes one Bristorian number, q (in B2, its three parts 1, i and j), and answers four questions about it, one for each letter. Each answer is a measured property of q: B its Tension, R its rotation, or its Reflection when it mirrors, O its order, T its brackets.

BRISTOR · ROTATION · ORDER · TORSIONtension: how squaring stretches a small ball · rotation or reflection: how it turns it · order: whether order matters · T, the associator: whether brackets matter

The project's short name was read this way in June 2026. It is a reading, not a derivation: the meanings came afterwards, and the name had room for them.

The numbers. A number here is x + yi + zj. Two of them multiply by the i-roll: i² = j² = −1, i·j = j and j·i = −i, with 1 the identity, extended to sums as usual. The product is neither commutative nor associative, so these are not the complex numbers or the quaternions. Squaring is (x + yi + zj)² = (x² − y² − z²) + y(2x − z)i + z(2x + y)j.

Roll the q sphere (in the corner of the setter, 's left or top frame, or at the top of on a narrow screen) like a trackball: the point under the pin is q. Its lines are cyan or orange by what squaring does at each direction (section 2 says what that is). All four cells read the same q, so all four change together. 1B: what squaring does to a small ball

Put a tiny ball round q and square every point. It comes out as an ellipsoid, drawn in the B cell: squaring stretches it more in some directions than in others. B is the longest stretch divided by the shortest, the ellipsoid's length l over its width w, both marked in the B cell: the tension squaring puts on the ball. 1 would mean the ball stays round.

In ℂ, B is always 1: complex squaring leaves small circles round. In B2 it depends on which way q points. At the conch, q = i, B is . At the thin one, q at (90°, 27°), it is , and the ball comes out nearly flat. Squaring changes sizes unevenly too: a number of size 1 squares to a size anywhere from to , by direction. In ℂ it is always 1.

2R: how far it turns, and whether it turns to its mirror

Take the stretch away and what is left is a turn. The R cell draws the axes 1, i and j before (grey) and after (coloured). R is the angle of that turn. In ℂ it is the angle of q.

In B2 the turn can also flip the ball over: a left-handed shape goes in, a right-handed one comes out. Which one happens is the hand of q. Squaring either keeps the hand (cyan) or mirrors it (orange); in ℂ it always keeps. A turn that keeps is a rotation. A turn that mirrors is a reflection: a rotation and a flip together. When it mirrors, the R cell also draws a dashed cyan frame: where the turn alone would take 1, i and j. The orange frame is that one flipped through the plane at right angles to the dashed axis; dotted lines link each pair, and they run along the axis. One number decides: det J, how squaring scales a tiny box. Above 0 it keeps; below 0 it mirrors. In standard terms, squaring preserves orientation at q where det J > 0 and reverses it where det J < 0. At the conch R is and it keeps; at (83°, 5°) R is and it mirrors.

maps the hand over every direction q can point. Across: the angle around from i toward j, −180° to 180°, i in the middle. Up and down: toward 1 and −1. Exactly half the directions mirror, because det J is odd: det J(−q) = −det J(q). Every line from 0 through the border makes the crossing cone (map ⇄ cone draws it). Two of its lines are named: the seam, along i + j, and the fold, along i − j. In the plane of i and j, det J = 4(y − z)(y + z)²: it changes sign across the seam and only touches zero at the fold.

R: the turn that is left after the stretch, and its hand: keep or mirror.

3O: does q·v equal v·q?

Multiply q by another number v, both ways round. In ℂ, q·v = v·q always. In B2 they can differ: i·j = j, but j·i = −i. The difference q·v − v·q is the commutator.

The O cell maps its size over every direction v, with the same axes as : brighter means order matters more, and the white rings mark the biggest. O is that biggest size, over |q|. At q = 1 it is : real numbers commute with everything. At the conch it is .

4T: does it matter where the brackets go?

Multiply three numbers: (q·i)·j, or q·(i·j). In ℂ and in the quaternions the two are always equal. In B2 they need not be. Their difference is the associator.

The T cell draws both products (green and violet) and the gap between them. T is that gap over |q|, for this one witness triple, q, i, j. At the conch it is ; at q = 1 it is .

The T cell also compares the two answers the way you would compare two complex numbers: by the angle between them, then × the ratio of their sizes. At the conch they come out the same size (ratio ) but turned . At (0°, 60°), toward 1, they point the same way () and the brackets change only the size: one is the size of the other. On the fold, (q·i)·j is 0. In ℂ the two are always equal: 0° and × 1.

In the name, Torsion stands for a defect: a familiar rule that fails here. T measures one defect, the brackets', and that measure is the associator. The torsion of differential geometry is the other defect, order: once a connection is built from this product, its torsion is the commutator, section 3's O.

5What the fractal cannot see

The Bristorbrot, the brot for short, is drawn by repeating one step, z ← z² + c. Adding c only moves things; the square does all of the shaping. And a square multiplies a number by itself:

  • It has nothing to put in order. z·z uses only the half of the product that does not care about order. O is invisible to it.
  • It has no brackets to move. One product of two numbers has nothing to regroup. T is invisible to it.
  • It does not mark a mirror. The fractal colours each point by one number: how many steps it takes to escape. That number records nothing about which way round a small shape came out at each step. So the fractal does not mark the mirror. Whether the mirror leaves any trace in the fractal's shape is open.

There is an exact test. Read every product the other way round, a·b as b·a. That gives a second algebra, the opposite algebra of B2. Its squares are the same, since q·q reads the same either way, so its fractal is the same at every point; section 9 checks this point by point. Yet its commutator is minus B2's, and its associator differs. Two different algebras draw one fractal, so the fractal cannot be what tells them apart. In standard terms: squaring's derivative is J(q)v = q·v + v·q, the half of the product that does not care about order. The commutator q·v − v·q is the other half, and the fractal never uses it.

So the fractal is a picture of B and of R's turn. now marks what it cannot see: O, T, and the mirror half of R. This instrument shows the other half of the name beside the fractal, one number at a time.

The fractal sees B and the turn. Only this instrument shows O, T and the mirror.

6B up close: the brotoid

The B cell squares a tiny ball. Grow the ball, and squaring bends it as well as stretching it. Start in ℂ: square every point of a circle and you get a limaçon. If the circle passes through 0, the curve gets a sharp point and becomes a cardioid (). The main body of the Mandelbrot set has this shape, turned and shifted (section 9 says how).

Now square a ball of radius r round q in B2. Its surface, the skin, lands on the brotoid (; q is the panel's q). Its lines are coloured by the hand where each point started. It does three things a squared circle never does:

  • Which way q points matters. Squared balls at different q are different shapes, not one shape turned, because B and R change with q.
  • It changes hand along a cone, not at a point. In ℂ squaring crushes the plane only at 0. In B2 it does so on the whole crossing cone, which this page also calls the brim: the ball is a hat's crown and the cone its brim. Where the ball, before squaring, meets that cone, it carries a rim; on the brotoid that rim is the crossing, where one hand gives way to the other. The brim inside the rim is the plate, and the hole it fills is the eye of the brim; squared, the plate is the pink and violet sheet on , pink where the brim's top faces you and violet where its underside does. The brotoid's crossing is also called the caustic.
  • It has a hand. Cyan where squaring keeps, orange where it mirrors, on the brotoid exactly as in .

Grow r and the brotoid passes through its moments (a moment is an r where the brotoid changes kind). ① The ball reaches the crossing cone, and the crossing is born on the brotoid. ② The skin starts to pass through itself, without cutting: a weft, with an umbrella at each end. Just before, the skin folds sharply where the weft will be born: a crease, ringed in cyan on with its gap, how far r still has to grow. ④ Part of a weft turns from cross to self. ③ At r = |q| the skin passes through 0. The buttons under set r just past each one, for the panel's q.

So what makes a brotoid?

BROT reads one number four ways: B, how squaring stretches a small ball; R, how it turns it or mirrors it; O, whether order matters; T, whether brackets do. Up close, B is a shape, the brotoid, and three things make it worth looking at. Its shape depends on which way q points: a different q gives a different shape, not the same shape turned. It has a hand, cyan where squaring keeps and orange where it mirrors. And it changes hand along a whole cone, so the crossing on the brotoid is a curve, not a point. As the ball grows, it passes through its moments, each a change of kind.

Only the direction of q comes back: q and three times q have the same four readings. And B, R, O and the hand cannot tell q from its fold mirror, its mirror image through the fold plane, because that mirror is the reversal automorphism: it keeps every product. T tells the two apart only because its test numbers, i and j, stay where they are while the mirror moves them. Tick leave T out to see the fold mirror come back as well.

9What it is not, and how we checked

  • It is not the Bristorbrot. It is one square, with no repeating and no c. The fractal comes from repeating the step thousands of times.
  • The cardioid link is ℂ's. In ℂ, a squared circle through 0 is the main body's edge turned half a turn and shifted: c = ¼ − z² carries one exactly onto the other. Whether anything like that holds in B2 is not known.
  • The pictures are samples. draws the skin from lines ( circles of latitude and meridians, points each); the weft and the umbrellas come from the closed form, not from the lines.

The square is (x + yi + zj)² = (x² − y² − z², y(2x − z), z(2x + y)) in (1, i, j), from the product on the Algebra page (i-roll: ea·ea = −1, ea·eb = sgn(b − a)·eb). Its derivative J and det J = 4(2x³ + x²y − x²z + 2xy² + 2xz² + y³ + y²z − yz² − z³) were found and factored symbolically.
