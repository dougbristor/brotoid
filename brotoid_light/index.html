Light and collapse

Two ways to look at the squared ball: run a bright light over its skin, and flatten it into a plane by multiplying it on one side.

A light over the surface

The light starts on the input ball and follows a looping path over it. Every point of the path is squared like the rest of the ball, so the light rides the brotoid's own skin, lifted a little along the surface's normal. Where the skin creases or folds back over itself, the highlight jumps or doubles. The shading uses the surface's tilt toward the light and fades with distance. Both sides of the skin are lit, because the skin turns inside out wherever squaring mirrors.

The tail is where the light has been. Set it to never and the path winds over the skin like a ball of wool, showing how the squaring stretches it. The tail keeps its last 30 000 points. Its colour is the hand of the input point the light was riding, so the light always knows its hand even where the skin does not. The whole ball at the origin has no single hand: x and −x square to the same point with opposite hands, so every point of that skin is covered twice, once by each hand. Squaring also doubles angles there, so the light laps the skin twice for each lap of the input ball. That is why it looks like it jumps. checked

The q setter (top left) chooses the ball's centre q. The sphere rolls under a fixed pin: drag it like a trackball, and the point of the sphere under the pin is the direction of q. The pin's length is |q| (one sphere radius is |q| = 1); set it with the wheel over the sphere or the |q| slider. The brotoid is rebuilt as you roll, and the panels further down catch up when you stop. The sphere's lines are coloured by the hand of q in each direction, cyan or orange, so the fold shows on the sphere as the border between the colours. The hand does not depend on |q|.

Light blur is a camera's shutter: the light is drawn along the path it ran during the frame, up to 32 copies added together, each rimmed in the hand it was riding. At 0 it is a sharp dot that hops from frame to frame. With weave it becomes a streak, capped at about 50 tail points of path.

Weave runs the light faster than the speed slider allows, ×4 to ×256, so the tail winds up quickly. Weave a full tail now lays all 30 000 points at once: the path costs about 1.5 µs a point, so the full tail takes about 45 ms. Drawing is the limit. While the view is still, the tail's drawn paths are kept and only the newest points are added, so a full tail draws as fast as no tail: 33 ms a frame against 49 ms when redrawn every frame (measured in turns). Turning the view, or turning slowly, redraws it every frame. The reused tail is pixel for pixel the redrawn one.

Collapse to rank 2

Multiply every point x of the brotoid by u, an imaginary of size 1: a point on the unit circle that runs from i through j (u = cos a·i + sin a·j). In this algebra the side u goes on matters:

  • u on the left, u·x: at u = i or j this is a rigid turn. The brotoid keeps its shape and its size.
  • u on the right, x·u: at u = i or j it flattens. Every number lands in the plane of 1 and u, a copy of ℂ, so the 3-D brotoid collapses to a flat picture (rank 2). proved

That is the split pair: rigid on the left, collapse on the right. The product of two units keeps the right-hand one, ea·eb = ±eb, so anything multiplied by u on the right can only come out along 1 or u.

algebra:

Bᵒᵖ is B read backwards: x∘y = y·x. Squaring reads the same both ways, so the brotoid and its light do not change. What changes is which side collapses. B keeps the right-hand unit (ea·eb = ±eb) and collapses on the right. Bᵒᵖ keeps the left-hand unit and collapses on the left. The panels below stay where they are: under Bᵒᵖ the flat picture moves to the left panel. The text on this page swaps every product and side to match, and the splats further down still show the side that collapses.

So the collapse to rank 2 by the right-hand product happens at i, at j and at the seam. Only at i and j is the left-hand product rigid at the same time. At the seam both sides flatten, onto one tilted plane, the plane perpendicular to 1 − i + j. proved

The collapse is a shadow. x·i throws every point along 1 + j onto the plane of 1 and i, stretching it by √2 one way: a camera view of the brotoid, written as a product.

Two flats as two splats

Take the flat results at u = i and at u = j, and draw each one as a single Gaussian splat. A splat is an ellipse with the centre and spread of the flattened brotoid. x·i lies in the plane of 1 and i, and x·j lies in the plane of 1 and j. So the two splats stand in two planes at right angles that share the 1 axis.

Each one is missing a term. Write x = a + b i + c j:

x·i = −b + (a − c) i + 0 j  · no j term · the direction 1 + j is lost
x·j = −c + 0 i + (a + b) j  · no i term · the direction 1 − i is lost

What one splat drops, the other keeps. Together, x ↦ (x·i, x·j) loses nothing: it has rank 3, and x can be read back from the pair. proved That holds for points, not for splats. The two wing Gaussians do not fix the bsplat. One symmetric matrix, H = [[2,−1,1],[−1,0,−1],[1,−1,0]], is invisible to both wings, so the bsplats C + tH all cast the same two wings. To rebuild the bsplat, keep it: the wings alone cannot. checked (caro) Doug's reading is that these two splats make the split pair. posed

Doug's structure for the splats is one bsplat, two wings and four splats:

  • The bsplat is the brotoid's own 3-D Gaussian, drawn dashed.
  • Its two shadows, through x·i and x·j, are the two wings.
  • Each wing splits again by hand. A cyan splat holds the skin that keeps its hand. An orange splat holds the skin that squaring mirrored, which on the x·j wing is folded inside the cyan.

A wing is exactly the mix of its two hand splats. Its spread is their two spreads, weighted by each one's share of the skin, plus the gap between their centres. checked

Brightness is density: each splat's peak is its share of the skin divided by its own area in the plane. A thin fold of orange shows up dense instead of vanishing.

The brotoid and its Jacobian

A splat is a small ball carried through the map that made the point. Squaring is the map here. Near the ball's centre q, squaring acts like its Jacobian J, so a small enough brotoid is just the ball stretched by J. Weighting the input sphere evenly, its spread is s²/3 · J·Jᵀ, the Jacobian ghost. As the ball grows, the brotoid parts from its ghost: it creases, folds over and splits by hand, and no single J can hold that. checked

insights' inverse walk runs the other way, pulling points back through the square root. Its splat is J⁻¹·J⁻ᵀ, the ghost of the inverse brotoid: the set of every x whose square lands in the ball. That set has one lobe for each real square root of the ball's centre. Here q² has 2, 4 or 6 of them, in ± pairs of opposite hand. Near the fold a lobe tears: the solver either fails to land a point or lands it far from J⁻¹'s guess. Such a point is marked torn and left out of the lobe's shape. It may be a jump to another root, but the page does not certify that (caro's audit found that the tears it tested were failed solves). checked

Chain n (caro's inverse_direction test). In the walk, q came from p₁ = q² + c, which came from p₂, and so on: p₁…pₙ are the points the walk just left. Pull a ball at pₙ back down that same path and you get the gold lobe. Its ghost is Mₙ = J(p₀)⁻¹·J(p₁)⁻¹·…·J(pₙ₋₁)⁻¹, the one-step ghosts multiplied. Each factor stretches much the same way again, so the long axis settles onto the direction the set runs. The path forward has one branch, so the chain never picks a root. Gold is drawn at the one-step lobe's volume. Raw, it would be far bigger or smaller, which is why caro keeps the one-step widths and borrows only the direction. c is fixed at caro's (.28, −.52, .18). checked

How we checked

Both determinants were found and factored symbolically from the product (i-roll, as on the brotoid page). The skeleton result behind the collapse, that right multiplication by a unit has rank 2 at every rank n, is proved in the canon (the diagonal-right skeleton, Theorem 1). This page draws only B2.
